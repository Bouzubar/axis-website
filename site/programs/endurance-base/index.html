Who it's for

Runners, cyclists and triathletes who want tissue that tolerates their training, and strength that makes every kilometre cheaper.

Endurance athletes get injured in a predictable way: the tissue fails before the engine does. Achilles and patellar tendons, shins, and the bones that take every landing are what end seasons, not a lack of fitness. And past a certain point, more volume stops improving performance and only adds risk. Heavy strength training addresses both. It improves running economy, the oxygen cost of a given pace, without adding a kilometre, and it builds the tendon and bone capacity that lets you keep training. Two sessions a week, about forty minutes each. It will not make you slow or bulky.

Where it sits in the year: Block 1 of 4. Base, Build, Compete, Restore. Run it in your base or off-season phase, when training volume is moderate and racing is far away.

Your week

Two gym days, and only two. That is deliberate. Two heavy sessions a week gives almost all of the benefit, and a third mostly costs recovery your main training needs.

When to lift: ideally on a hard training day rather than an easy one, at least six hours after your session, or on the easiest day of the week. Never the day before a key workout or long effort.

Every session runs the same way:

  1. Warm-up, 8–10 min. Short, and enough to lift heavy safely.
  2. Main lifts, 20–25 min. The first two or three exercises, with long rests. This is where force is built.
  3. Tendon work, 8–10 min. Calf raises, Nordics and tibialis work. Last, and never cut.
  4. Leave. Thirty-five to forty-five minutes in total. If it is running towards an hour, the volume is too high.

Heavy lifting happens while you are fresh, and tendon work sits at the end where it survives a rushed session. This block supports your training. It should never be the reason a key session goes badly.

How the 12 weeks progress

Three phases of four weeks each. Load climbs and reps fall across the twelve weeks. Volume stays deliberately low, so the gym never competes with your running, riding or swimming. Tendon and bone adapt to load, not to mileage. Running builds the engine but does comparatively little for the tissue that has to survive it, and cyclists and swimmers, who get no impact loading at all, need this work most. Heavy slow calf raises and hamstring eccentrics appear in every session.

Deload weeks: weeks 6 and 12 halve the sets, and you retest fresh. Take a deload early if your training block spikes.

The rules that make it work

  • Heavy loads, low volume, long rests. Three to six reps, three to four sets, two to three minutes between sets. Fatigue is not the goal, force is. Your sport already supplies all the fatigue you can absorb.
  • Never before a key session or long effort. Lift on a hard day, at least six hours apart, or on your easiest day. Protect the workouts that decide your race.
  • Tendon work is not optional. Heavy slow calf raises and hamstring eccentrics are in every session, because Achilles and hamstring problems are what end endurance seasons.
  • It will not make you bulky. Heavy, low-rep training builds force and tendon stiffness with very little size. Body weight should stay roughly stable across the block. If it climbs, that is nutrition, not the barbell.
  • Two sessions is the whole prescription. A third almost always costs more in recovery than it adds in strength.
  • Count your landings, not just your sets. Every jump, hop, bound and drop is a landing your tendons pay for, warm-up hops included. Keep the week under about 120 landings in this block, with your own training counted on top.
Terms
  • Block: 12 weeks. This program. One block develops one set of qualities to a set standard.
  • Phase (mesocycle): 4 weeks. Each one targets one quality. Four weeks is a choice, not a rule. The length follows the goal.
  • Training week (microcycle): where effort climbs, sets are added and the deload lands.
  • Training year (macrocycle): four blocks in order (Base, Build, Compete, Restore), about 48 weeks.

How you'll measure progress

Weeks 6 and 12 are retest weeks, done fresh at the end of each deload. Every week also starts with a 90-second check: three standing broad jumps at the start of Day 1, keeping the best. More than 10 per cent below your own rolling average means that day is reduced. The power work comes out and each lift loses one set, whatever the page says.

What good progress looks like

  • Weeks 1–4: you will be sore, and easy runs will feel heavy for about two weeks. That settles, and it is not a sign the training is wrong.
  • Weeks 5–8: strength climbs quickly, and the calf and hamstring numbers move fast.
  • Weeks 9–12: the same pace starts to feel easier at the same effort. That is economy improving.

Body weight should stay roughly stable throughout. The block builds force, not mass.

Respect the week

Count everything: races, training sessions, gym and sleep. Sudden jumps in weekly workload are the most reliable predictor of injury in amateur sport. In a heavy training week, drop a gym session or cut the power work, but never cut both the training and the recovery. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep. It is the most powerful performance tool available, and the cheapest.

The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.

Strength training cuts sports injuries to under a third of the control rate. Stretching does not reduce them. Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen, Br J Sports Med 2014. Meta-analysis of 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants. PMID 24100287 Risk ratio 0.32 for strength training and 0.96 for stretching. The trials varied a lot, and dose was not standardised.
Programs that include the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halve hamstring injuries. van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley, Br J Sports Med 2019. Meta-analysis, 15 studies, 8,459 athletes. PMID 30808663 Risk ratio 0.49. The effect shrinks but survives when the eight high-risk-of-bias studies are removed (0.55). It measures programs that include the exercise, not the exercise alone.
Every sensible prescription beats not training. Heavier loads rank highest for strength. Multiple sets rank highest for muscle size. Currier et al., Br J Sports Med 2023. Network meta-analysis, 178 strength and 119 hypertrophy studies. PMID 37414459 The differences between prescriptions were small, and all of them worked.
