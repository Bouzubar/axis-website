Who this is for

Athletes who have had a forward (anterior) shoulder dislocation, with or without surgery, have finished physiotherapy, and want to get back to contact or overhead sport.

Dislocations happen with the arm up and back. That is exactly the position most programs never strengthen, so this one builds strength there step by step, then speed, then your sport's positions.

Not for: two or more dislocations without a surgeon's opinion, known bone loss in the socket or ball, a dislocation that came with a fracture, backward (posterior) dislocations, shoulders that are loose in many directions, or anyone 40 or older who still can't lift the arm well after a first dislocation, because a rotator cuff tear needs to be ruled out first.

Under 25 and playing a contact sport? After a first dislocation, the shoulder is likely to dislocate again at this age. See a shoulder surgeon before choosing to recover without surgery.

Your week

Three gym days a week, with a rest day between each.

How it runs:

  1. Entry Check first. If any line is not true, you don't start.
  2. Test before Week 1. Stability, power, strength and range tests, plus a confidence questionnaire.
  3. Check after every session. A pain table tells you whether to progress, hold or step back.
  4. Pass each gate to move on. If you don't pass, you repeat the last 2 weeks and retest.
  5. Pick your sport option in Phase 3. Contact (rugby, combat sports, football goalkeepers) or Overhead (throwing, racket sports, volleyball, swimming).

The rules that make it work

  • Range comes before speed. The shoulder earns each new position slowly before it is asked to move fast there.
  • Any slipping resets the plan. A feeling of the shoulder slipping means you stop the end-range and fast work and go back to Phase 1 loads.
  • Tests decide, not the calendar.

How you'll measure progress

At Week 0, Week 4, Week 8 and Week 12 you test:

  • Stability: a push-up position touch test (the CKCUEST).
  • Power: a seated single-arm medicine ball throw, each arm.
  • Strength: side-lying external rotation reps, each arm.
  • Range: a filmed rotation check with the arm out to the side.
  • Confidence: the SIRSI questionnaire, a published 12-question scale of how ready you feel.

Exit standard at Week 12: power and rotation strength at 90% or more of the other arm, 21 or more touches on the stability test, range within 10° of the other side, no feeling of slipping with the arm up and back, and a SIRSI score of 55 or more. The 18 and 21 touch targets and the SIRSI 55 are AXIS working targets, not published cut-offs. For contact sports, the final decision to return to full contact is made with your surgeon or physiotherapist.

The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.

European experts agreed that return to sport after a shoulder dislocation should be based on pain-free full range, stability, strength and sport readiness, usually reached 4 to 6 months after surgery depending on the procedure and sport. Most recommendations rest on moderate or low-level evidence.
How ready athletes feel can be measured. On the SIRSI scale, rugby players who returned to their sport scored about 61, against 38 for those who did not. Gerometta, Klouche, Herman et al., Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy 2018. Validation study, 62 athletes. PMID 28707114 Mostly male rugby players. Before you buy: you must pass this entry check

All of these must be true before you start:

  • Discharged by your physiotherapist, or cleared by your surgeon to start strength training.
  • After surgery: the time your surgeon set has passed. Without surgery: at least 6 weeks since the dislocation.
  • Full pain-free range, or within 10° of the other side, lifting the arm forward, out to the side and turning it out.
  • 10 push-ups (from the knees is fine) without pain or a feeling the shoulder will slip.
  • No feeling of the shoulder slipping in daily life, and no numbness on the outside of the upper arm.

Stop and contact your surgeon or physiotherapist if the shoulder slips out or partly out, the arm goes "dead" for a moment, you feel numbness or tingling down the arm, a clunk with pain, or pain above 5/10 that lasts into the next day.
