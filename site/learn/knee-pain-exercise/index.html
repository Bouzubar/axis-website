Pain at the front of the knee: why the right exercise beats rest

Rest calms the knee for a week. Exercise is what gives it the capacity to stay calm.

The short answer

For pain around the kneecap, an international expert panel recommends exercise therapy, especially hip and knee exercises together. Moderate pain during training can be fine if it settles by the next morning.

Most knee pain in active people is not damage. It is a capacity problem: the tissue around the kneecap is asked to handle more than it has been trained for. Rest lowers the demand, so the pain fades. Then you train again, the demand comes back, and so does the pain, because rest never raised the capacity.

What the research recommends

In 2018, 41 experts in kneecap pain (patellofemoral pain) reviewed the trials and voted on what works. Their clearest recommendation was exercise therapy, and especially hip exercises and knee exercises done together. Foot orthoses and combined treatments also helped some people.

They did not recommend treatments done on their own that do nothing to build strength, such as joint mobilisations or machines like ultrasound. Taping, bracing, dry needling and massage-type treatments were rated as uncertain.

Why the hip matters for a knee problem

The knee sits between the hip and the ankle, and both steer it. When the glutes don't hold the thigh in line, the knee drops inward on squats, stairs and landings, and it takes force the hip should have taken. That is why a good knee program trains the hip from day one.

Can I train if it hurts a little?

Often, yes. In a trial of 38 people with Achilles tendon pain, one group kept running and jumping, guided by a simple pain rule. Pain up to 5/10 during activity was allowed, as long as it settled by the next morning and did not build from week to week. They recovered just as well as the group that stopped.

Be honest about the limits. That trial was about the Achilles tendon, not the kneecap, and a 2020 review found that most tendon programs use a pain rule like this, but no study has shown which rule is best. Treat it as a sensible guide, not a law.

When to get checked first

See a doctor or physiotherapist before training if the pain started with a specific injury (a pop, the knee giving way, or swelling within hours), or if you have locking, numbness, fever, or pain that wakes you at night.
