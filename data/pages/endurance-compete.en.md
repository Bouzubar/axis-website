## Who it's for

Runners, cyclists and triathletes in their racing season, who want to finish the season as strong as they started it.

This is the block where most endurance athletes make the same mistake: they stop lifting once racing starts. Strength drops measurably within a few weeks of stopping, and by mid-season they are back where they began, with the tendon capacity gone at exactly the point in the year when racing is most frequent and most intense. The answer is not to keep training hard. It is to keep training heavy, with almost no volume: three working sets, three reps, done in about thirty minutes. Enough to hold everything the Base and Build blocks built, and light enough that your legs never notice.

**Where it sits in the year:** Block 3 of 4. Base, Build, Compete, Restore. Run it through your racing season, from your first race to your last. The goal is maintenance, not gains.

## Your week

Two short gym sessions in a normal race-season week, and one in a race week.

**Normal week**

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Session | **Day 2** | Session | Session | Rest | Long session |

**Race week**

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Session | Session | Rest | Shakeout | Rest | Race |

In a race week the one session comes early, with nothing heavy and no plyometrics inside 72 hours of the start.

**Every session runs the same way:**
1. **Warm-up, 6–8 min.** The usual sequence, shortened. You are already race-fit.
2. **Plyometrics, 3 min.** A few sharp contacts to keep the spring. Never in the 72 hours before a race.
3. **Main lifts, 12–15 min.** Two lifts, three working sets each, heavy and fast. Nothing to failure, ever.
4. **Tendon work, 5 min.** Calf raises and tibialis work. The one thing that stays in every session.

If a race-season gym session is running to forty-five minutes, it is too long. The extra time buys nothing and costs you a good workout later in the week.

## How the 12 weeks progress

Losing strength is faster than building it, but holding it is far cheaper. Meaningful losses in maximal strength appear within a few weeks of stopping, and tendon stiffness follows the same pattern. Yet keeping those qualities needs only a fraction of the work that built them. Cutting volume hard while keeping the load high preserves strength. Cutting the intensity is what loses it. The other half of this block is timing. Heavy lifting leaves the nervous system and muscles tired for roughly one to three days depending on the dose, which is why the prescription is small and nothing heavy goes near race day.

Three phases of four weeks each. Load holds, effort eases, and everything thins out towards your key race.

| | Weeks 1–4 | Weeks 5–8 | Weeks 9–12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase** | Strength maintenance | Rate of force | Race freshness |
| **Goal** | Hold maximal strength during the racing season | Hold rate of force development during the racing season | Shed accumulated fatigue before key races |
| **Looks like** | Three working sets, well short of failure. It feels easy, by design | Same load, even less of it. Bar speed matters more than the number | One session in any race week, done early |
| **Working sets per week** | 30 | 30 | 24 |
| **Average effort** | RPE 7.1, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 6.9, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 6.0, about 4 reps in reserve |
| **Landings per week** | 65 | 69 | 65 |

RPE is effort rated out of 10. Reps in reserve is how many more good reps you could have done, so "about 3 reps in reserve" means about 3 reps left in the tank.

**Lighter weeks:** the light weeks are built to sit around races. Move them to match your actual race calendar. The calendar wins.

### The rules that make it work
- **Three working sets per lift is the whole session.** Maintenance needs a fraction of the work that built the strength. Three sets of three, twice a week, holds what the Base and Build blocks gave you.
- **Keep the load, cap the volume.** Intensity preserves strength, not sets. Lifting light and often is the version that loses everything by mid-season.
- **Nothing heavy, and no hopping, inside 72 hours of a race.** Heavy lifting leaves fatigue for one to three days. Do the session early in the week and forget about it.
- **Never to failure, all season.** Every set finishes with reps in reserve. Race season is not the time to find your limit.
- **Calf work never comes out.** Achilles problems are what end endurance seasons. Three sets of heavy slow calf raises, every session, whatever else gets cut.
- **Count your landings, not just your sets.** Every jump, hop, bound and drop is a landing your tendons pay for, warm-up hops included. Keep the week under about 100 landings in season, with your own training counted on top.

### Terms (expandable)
- **Block:** 12 weeks. This program. One block develops one set of qualities to a set standard.
- **Phase (mesocycle):** 4 weeks. Each one targets one quality. Four weeks is a choice, not a rule. The length follows the goal.
- **Training week (microcycle):** where effort climbs, sets are added and the deload lands.
- **Training year (macrocycle):** four blocks in order (Base, Build, Compete, Restore), about 48 weeks.

## How you'll measure progress

Every week starts with a 90-second check: three standing broad jumps at the start of Day 1, keeping the best. More than 10 per cent below your own rolling average means that day is reduced. The power work comes out and each lift loses one set, whatever the page says.

**What good progress looks like**
- **Weeks 1–4:** the sessions feel almost too easy. That is correct. Resist adding sets.
- **Weeks 5–8:** this is where athletes drift into skipping the gym. The ones who don't are the ones still healthy in the last month of the season.
- **Weeks 9–12:** volume thins further into your key race.

The success measure for this block is a flat line, not a rising one: as strong at your last race as at your first.

## Respect the week

Count everything: races, training sessions, gym and sleep. Sudden jumps in weekly workload are the most reliable predictor of injury in amateur sport. In a heavy training week, drop a gym session or cut the power work, but never cut both the training and the recovery. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep. It is the most powerful performance tool available, and the cheapest.

## The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.
