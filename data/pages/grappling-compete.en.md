## Who it's for

Grapplers heading into a competition camp. Most competitors lose the fight in camp, not on the mat. Mat volume goes up, gym time disappears, sleep gets worse, and the athlete arrives at the venue in the worst condition of their year, then blames the taper.

This block takes the opposite approach. Two short gym sessions a week protect what Base and Build produced, arranged so they never compete with hard mat work. Then a genuine taper cuts the fatigue while the loads stay heavy enough to keep you strong. Nothing new is introduced. The last four weeks are about being fresh, not about proving anything in the weights room.

**Where it sits in the year:** Block 3 of 4. Base, Build, Compete, Restore, run in order. Run it through your competition camp. If the camp is shorter than twelve weeks, start at the phase that matches the time you have left.

## Your week

Two gym days, on your lighter mat days, never within four hours of hard sparring. If a gym session and a hard round compete for the same energy, the round wins.

**Camp week, 2 gym days**

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Mat | **Day 2** | Mat | Mat | Rest | Mat |

**Fight week, 1 short day**

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Mat | Rest | Mat, light | Rest | Weigh-in | Compete |

There is no Day 3 in a camp. In fight week, do Day 1 only, at half the sets, early in the week. Nothing heavy inside 72 hours of competing.

**Every session runs the same way:**
1. **Warm-up, 8–10 min.** The same sequence each time. Glutes, hips and shoulders switched on before anything fast.
2. **Speed and power, 12–15 min**, in the phases that include it. A few fast throws or jumps, full recovery, stopped the moment quality drops.
3. **Strength, 20–25 min.** The heavy compound lifts, three working sets each, with two to three minutes between sets.
4. **Tissue work, 8–10 min.** Last, and never cut.

## How the 12 weeks progress

Three phases of four weeks each. Strength and power are lost quickly, and a hard camp speeds that up. Two brief heavy sessions a week are enough to hold almost everything. They are not enough for progress, and that is fine. The aim is to arrive with what you already built. Loads stay heavy, working sets hold at three per lift, and grip and neck work continue every week until fight week.

| | Weeks 1–4 | Weeks 5–8 | Weeks 9–12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase** | Strength maintenance | Neural drive | Speed and freshness |
| **Goal** | Hold maximal strength through a hard camp. Three heavy sets per lift and out. | The heaviest loads of the camp, three crisp sets per lift | Shed accumulated fatigue before competition. Effort comes down, loads stay respectable. |
| **Working sets per week** | 36 | 38 | 34 |
| **Average effort** | RPE 7.3, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 7.9, about 2 reps in reserve | RPE 6.5, about 3 to 4 reps in reserve |
| **Landings per week** | 40 | 40 | 40 |
| **Throws per week** | 12 | 0 | 9 |

RPE is a 1–10 rating of how hard a set feels. Reps in reserve is how many more good reps you could have done, so about 2 means 2 reps left in the tank.

**Deload and taper:** week 6 is a normal deload, and you can take it early if the camp spikes. Weeks 9–12 bring effort and fatigue down step by step into competition, and the last week is almost nothing.

### The rules that make it work
- **Three heavy sets per lift is a full session.** Maintenance needs load more than volume. Well over an hour in the gym during a camp takes energy the mat needs.
- **Never lift heavy within four hours of hard sparring,** and nothing heavy at all inside 72 hours of competition. A session that costs you a round has already failed.
- **The jump number outranks the plan.** If your jump is more than 10% below your average, reduce that day, whatever the page says. Camp fatigue builds faster than anyone believes.
- **Nothing new after week 4.** No new exercises, no new maxes, no experiments in the last eight weeks. Novelty brings soreness, and soreness in camp costs mat time.
- **A taper should feel like undertraining.** Feeling twitchy and under-worked in the last week is the intended result.
- **Count landings, not just sets.** Every jump, hop, bound and drop is a landing your tendons pay for, warm-up hops included. Keep the week under about 100 landings in season, with sport practice on top.

### Terms (expandable)
- **Training year (macrocycle):** four blocks in order (Base, Build, Compete, Restore), about 48 weeks.
- **Block:** 12 weeks. This program. One block develops one set of qualities to a set standard.
- **Phase (mesocycle):** 4 weeks. Each one targets one quality. Four weeks is a choice, not a rule. The length follows the goal.
- **Training week (microcycle):** where effort climbs, sets are added and the deload lands.

## How you'll measure progress

The weekly jump number is the main gauge. In camp, a flat line is a good result.

**What good progress looks like**
- **Weeks 1–4:** the gym feels almost too easy while the mat gets harder. That balance is deliberate.
- **Weeks 5–8:** strength holds and the jump number stays flat, which in camp is a win.
- **Weeks 9–12:** effort drops and you start to feel springy instead of heavy.

The goal is to arrive fresh, not to arrive tested. If your numbers are climbing in the final month, the camp is too easy or the gym is too hard.

## Respect the week

Count everything: matches, sport training, gym and sleep. Sudden jumps in weekly workload are the most reliable predictor of injury in amateur sport. In a heavy sport week, drop a gym session or cut the power work, never both the sport and the recovery. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep. It is the most powerful performance tool you have, and the cheapest.

## The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.

| Finding | Source | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Strength training cuts sports injuries to under a third of the control rate. Stretching does not reduce them. | Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen, *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2014. Meta-analysis of 25 randomised controlled trials, 26,610 participants. PMID 24100287 | Risk ratio 0.32 for strength training and 0.96 for stretching. The trials varied a lot, and dose was not standardised. |
| Programs that include the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halve hamstring injuries. | van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley, *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2019. Meta-analysis, 15 studies, 8,459 athletes. PMID 30808663 | Risk ratio 0.49, or 0.55 with the eight high-risk-of-bias studies removed. It measures programs that include the exercise, not the exercise alone. |
| One adductor exercise, three times a week in pre-season and once a week in season, cut reported groin problems by 41%. | Harøy et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2019. Cluster-randomised controlled trial, 35 teams, 652 male footballers. PMID 29891614 | Odds ratio 0.59. Self-reported problems in semi-professional men's football only. It has not been repeated in women or in other sports. |
| Every sensible prescription beats not training. Heavier loads rank highest for strength. Multiple sets rank highest for muscle size. | Currier, McLeod, Banfield et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2023. Network meta-analysis, 178 strength and 119 hypertrophy studies. PMID 37414459 | The differences between prescriptions were small, and all of them worked. |
