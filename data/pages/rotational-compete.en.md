## Who it's for

Padel, tennis and squash players in their competitive season. Racket players train hardest in the off-season and then, just as the overhead volume triples, stop. That is backwards. The shoulder is under the most load during the season, and it has the least support then.

This block is two short sessions a week that do three things and nothing else: keep the strength you built, keep the rotator cuff and mid-back working, and keep the lunge strong. Everything else is stripped out on purpose. It is not tiring, and it is what stops the second half of your season looking worse than the first.

**Where it sits in the year:** Block 3 of 4 in the Rotational family. Base, Build, Compete, Restore, in that order. Run it through your competitive season. If the season runs longer than twelve weeks, repeat weeks 9–12 until it ends.

## Your week

Two gym days is the standard version. In a tournament week, Day 1 alone. It carries the heavy lift and the shoulder work, the two things that must not lapse.

| | Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **In-season, 2 days** | Rest | **Day 1** | Court | **Day 2** | Court | Rest | Match |
| **Tournament week, 1 day** | Rest | **Day 1** | Court | Match | Match | Rest | Match |

Never train within 24 hours of a match. Move the gym days to fit your own fixtures.

**Every session runs the same way:**
1. **Warm-up, 8–10 min.** The same sequence every session. Glutes, hips and shoulders switched on before anything fast happens.
2. **Speed and power, 12–15 min.** Med-ball throws, rotational power and lateral jumps, short and sharp, with full recovery between sets. The set stops the moment quality drops.
3. **Strength, 20–25 min.** The heavy compound lifts, with two to three minutes between sets.
4. **Tissue work, 8–10 min.** External rotation, Y raises and rows for the shoulder, plus Copenhagens and Nordics. Last, and never cut.

Sessions are short on purpose, 48–60 minutes. If a session leaves the shoulder sore for the next match, it was too long, not too light.

## How the 12 weeks progress

In-season training has one job: keep what you built while the sport takes priority. For racket players that means heavy loads at very low volume, the rotator cuff and mid-back trained every week whatever the fixture list says, and the lunge kept strong so the legs are not the reason you lose late. It does not mean progression. Chasing gym numbers mid-season is how players arrive at matches already tired.

| | Weeks 1–4 | Weeks 5–8 | Weeks 9–12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase** | Strength maintenance | Power maintenance | Shoulder durability |
| **Goal** | Keep maximal strength through competition weeks | Keep throwing-shoulder power through competition weeks | Keep rotator cuff strength and shoulder tissue tolerance |
| **Looks like** | Three heavy sets per lift and the cuff work in full | The same heavy, low-volume strength, with fewer, sharper throws | Volume drops again, the cuff work stays, nothing new is introduced |
| **Working sets a week** | 36 | 38 | 36 |
| **Average effort** | RPE 7.0, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 7.3, about 2.7 in reserve | RPE 7.1, about 2.9 in reserve |
| **Landings a week** | 40 | 40 | 40 |
| **Throws a week** | 42 | 16 | 42 |

RPE is effort rated from 1 to 10. RPE 7 means about 3 reps left in the tank. Working sets exclude the warm-up.

**Deload weeks:** weeks 6 and 12 halve the sets, and week 12 is a full deload. In a tournament run, take the deload early. The calendar decides, not the page.

### The rules that make it work
- **Heavy, but very little of it.** Three heavy sets per lift at RPE 7–8. Heavy load maintains strength. Volume makes you sore, and sore costs you matches.
- **The cuff work is the point of this block.** External rotation, Y raises and rows run every session of all twelve weeks. If you cut one thing, never cut this.
- **Never within 24 hours of a match.** A session that compromises a match has failed, however good it felt.
- **Watch the external-rotation hold, not the pain.** When the racket-side hold starts dropping, reduce court volume that week. Waiting for pain means waiting until it is already an injury.
- **Don't chase personal bests.** If your lifts are climbing hard during a season, the gym is taking energy the sport needs. Holding the line is success here.
- **A jump number is the cheapest fatigue test there is.** Three standing broad jumps at the start of every Day 1, keeping the best. More than 10% below your own rolling average means reduce that day: drop the power work and one set from each lift.
- **Count landings, not just sets.** Every jump, hop, bound and drop counts, warm-up hops included. Throws, sprints, step-overs and split steps do not. Keep the week under about 100 landings in season. Matches and court sessions count on top.

### Terms (expandable)
- **Block:** 12 weeks. This program. One block develops one set of qualities to a set standard.
- **Phase (mesocycle):** 4 weeks. Each one targets one quality. Four weeks is a choice, not a rule. The length follows the goal.
- **Training week (microcycle):** where effort climbs, sets are added and the deload lands.
- **Training year (macrocycle):** four blocks in order (Base, Build, Compete, Restore), about 48 weeks.

## How you'll measure progress

In season, two numbers matter: the racket-side external-rotation (ER) hold and the weekly jump number. You retest in the deload weeks, 6 and 12.

**What good progress looks like**
- **Weeks 1–4:** the gym stops feeling like an extra burden, and the shoulder settles between matches.
- **Weeks 5–8:** the ER hold stays flat instead of falling. Mid-season, that is a win.
- **Weeks 9–12:** you finish the season serving as well as you started it.

**Block done when:** the season is over, you are serving as well as you were at the start, and the shoulder hold never fell. Holding the line is the objective. If your numbers are climbing hard, you are training too much for a season, and it will show on court.

## Respect the week

Count everything: matches, court sessions, gym and sleep. Sudden jumps in weekly workload are the most reliable predictor of injury in amateur sport. In a heavy match week, drop a gym session or cut the power work, but never cut both the sport and the recovery. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep. It is the most powerful performance tool available, and the cheapest.

## The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.

| Finding | Source | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Strength training cuts sports injuries to under a third of the control rate. Stretching does not reduce them. | Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen, *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2014. Meta-analysis of 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants. PMID 24100287 | Risk ratio 0.32 for strength training and 0.96 for stretching. The trials varied a lot, and dose was not standardised. |
| Every sensible prescription beats not training. Heavier loads rank highest for strength. Multiple sets rank highest for muscle size. | Currier, McLeod, Banfield et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2023. Network meta-analysis, 178 strength and 119 hypertrophy studies. PMID 37414459 | The differences between prescriptions were small, and all of them worked. |
| Programs that include the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halve hamstring injuries. | van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley, *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2019. Meta-analysis, 15 studies, 8,459 athletes. PMID 30808663 | Risk ratio 0.49, or 0.55 with the high-risk-of-bias studies removed. It measures programs that include the exercise, not the exercise alone. |
| One adductor exercise, three times a week in pre-season and once a week in season, cut reported groin problems by 41%. | Harøy et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 2019. Cluster-randomised trial, 35 teams, 652 male footballers. PMID 29891614 | Odds ratio 0.59. Self-reported, semi-professional men's football only. Not yet repeated in women or other sports. |
