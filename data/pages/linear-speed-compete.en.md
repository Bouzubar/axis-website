## Who it's for

Sprinters, jumpers and hurdlers in their competitive season, who want to be fast on the day that counts rather than in midweek training.

Track seasons are lost to accumulated fatigue and to hamstrings that go in week five. By competition season the fitness is already built. Nothing done now makes you faster, and almost everything added makes you more tired. So this block does very little, very well: two short gym sessions to hold strength, sprint work kept sharp and brief, and volume that steps down as the meets get more important. The word that governs everything here is freshness. An athlete who arrives at the final rested and slightly undertrained beats the same athlete arriving fit and flat.

**Where it sits in the year:** Block 3 of 4. Base, Build, Compete, Restore. Run it through your competitive season, however many meets it holds.

## Your week

Two gym days of 48–60 minutes, on non-track days or straight after track work, and never the day before a meet.

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Track | **Day 2** | Track | Rest | Track | Rest |

**Meet weeks:** Day 1 only, at half the sets, early in the week. Nothing heavy inside 72 hours of competing.

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rest | **Day 1** | Track | Rest | Track, light | Rest | Meet |

**Every session runs the same way:**
1. **Warm-up, 8–10 min.** The same sequence each time. Glutes, hips and shoulders switched on before anything fast happens.
2. **Speed and power, 12–15 min.** Sprints, jumps and throws, with full recovery between sets. The set stops the moment quality drops.
3. **Strength, 20–25 min.** The heavy compound lifts, with two to three minutes between sets.
4. **Tissue work, 8–10 min.** Nordics and calves. Last, and never cut.

Speed and power come before fatigue, and heavy lifts before assistance. Tissue work goes last so it survives a rushed session instead of being cut from the middle.

## How the 12 weeks progress

Three phases of four weeks each. Speed and strength can both be held on a fraction of the work it took to build them, so two brief sessions a week holds almost everything. The risk in season is not losing fitness. It is accumulated fatigue. Quality rises while volume falls, and fast running never stops, because top speed fades within about two weeks without it.

| | Weeks 1–4 | Weeks 5–8 | Weeks 9–12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase** | Strength maintenance | Speed maintenance | Peak freshness |
| **Goal** | Hold maximal strength and sprint speed during competition | Hold maximal sprint velocity on less training volume | Shed accumulated fatigue before target competitions |
| **Looks like** | Three heavy sets per lift, two sharp sprints, done | The same work, faster and shorter. Rest goes up, reps come down | Almost nothing, done perfectly. Volume falls hard while intensity stays high |
| **Working sets per week** | 36 | 37 | 34 |
| **Average effort** | RPE 6.9, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 7.0, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 6.2, about 4 reps in reserve |
| **Landings per week** | 32 | 32 | 18 |
| **Throws per week** | 12 | 12 | 9 |

RPE is effort rated out of 10. Reps in reserve is how many more good reps you could have done, so "about 3 reps in reserve" means about 3 reps left in the tank.

**Lighter weeks:** week 6 is a normal light week. From week 9 the volume is stripped back into your key competitions, and week 12 is your competition week.

### The rules that make it work
- **The fast metres come down in season.** Roughly 150–250 metres above 90 per cent effort across the week, and none of it inside 72 hours of a meet. Racing supplies the top-end exposure.
- **Nothing heavy inside 72 hours of a meet.** And nothing new inside three weeks of one. Meet weeks get Day 1 only, at half the sets, early in the week.
- **Keep sprinting, cut the reps.** Top speed fades within about two weeks without exposure, so fast running never stops. Three good reps is a full session in this block.
- **Do not chase training times.** Personal bests belong at meets. Running your fastest in midweek training means the taper is wrong and the season will peak in the wrong month.
- **Nordics continue all season.** Reduced, but never removed. In season, sprint exposure is at its highest and hamstring capacity is least protected.
- **Count your landings, not just your sets.** Every jump, hop, bound and drop is a landing your tendons pay for, warm-up hops included. Throws and sprints are not landings. Keep the week under about 100 landings in season, with sport practice counted on top.

### Terms (expandable)
- **Block:** 12 weeks. This program. One block develops one set of qualities to a set standard.
- **Phase (mesocycle):** 4 weeks. Each one targets one quality. Four weeks is a choice, not a rule. The length follows the goal.
- **Training week (microcycle):** where effort climbs, sets are added and the deload lands.
- **Training year (macrocycle):** four blocks in order (Base, Build, Compete, Restore), about 48 weeks.

## How you'll measure progress

The broad jump outranks the plan. Three standing broad jumps take ninety seconds and are the cheapest fatigue check in the sport. More than 10 per cent below your own average means volume comes out that week.

**What good progress looks like**
- **Weeks 1–4:** the gym feels light and the track feels sharp. Both are intentional.
- **Weeks 5–8:** times hold or improve slightly while total work falls.
- **Weeks 9–12:** you feel almost undertrained, and run your fastest.

Training personal bests in the middle of the season are a warning sign. Those should be happening at meets.

## Respect the week

Count everything: meets, track sessions, gym and sleep. Sudden jumps in weekly workload are the most reliable predictor of injury in amateur sport. In a heavy week, drop a gym session or cut the power work, but never cut both the sport and the recovery. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep. It is the most powerful performance tool available, and the cheapest.

## The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.

| Finding | Source | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Programs that include the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halve hamstring injuries. | van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley, *Br J Sports Med* 2019. Meta-analysis, 15 studies, 8,459 athletes. PMID 30808663 | Risk ratio 0.49. The effect shrinks but survives when the eight high-risk-of-bias studies are removed (0.55). It measures programs that include the exercise, not the exercise alone. |
| Strength training cuts sports injuries to under a third of the control rate. Stretching does not reduce them. | Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen, *Br J Sports Med* 2014. Meta-analysis of 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants. PMID 24100287 | Risk ratio 0.32 for strength training and 0.96 for stretching. The trials varied a lot, and dose was not standardised. |
| Every sensible prescription beats not training. Heavier loads rank highest for strength. Multiple sets rank highest for muscle size. | Currier et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2023. Network meta-analysis, 178 strength and 119 hypertrophy studies. PMID 37414459 | The differences between prescriptions were small, and all of them worked. |
