## Who it's for

Sprinters, jumpers and hurdlers whose competitive season has just finished.

Sprinting and jumping are the highest-impact things a body does voluntarily. A season of it leaves Achilles tendons, patellar tendons, shins and hamstrings carrying damage that never gets a chance to resolve, because the next session is always three days away. This block is the only window in the year where impact genuinely stops: four weeks with no sprinting at all, then a staged return. It is also the only block where getting bigger and stronger is the priority rather than a side effect, because muscle built now is speed available later in the year.

**Where it sits in the year:** Block 4 of 4. Base, Build, Compete, Restore, then back to Base. Start it as soon as your final competition is done.

**Before week 1, take two weeks completely off.** No track, no lifting, no jumping. Walk, swim, sleep. It is prescribed, not optional, and it is the part most athletes skip.

## Your week

Three non-consecutive days, done in order. Sessions are longer than in the Build and Compete blocks, at far lower intensity.

| Sun | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Rest | **Day 2** | Rest | **Day 3** | Rest | Rest |

**Short version:** Day 1 and Day 2 only, with at least two rest days between them.

**Every session runs the same way:**
1. **Warm-up, 8–10 min.** The same sequence each time. Glutes, hips and shoulders switched on first.
2. **Speed and power, 12–15 min.** Jumps and throws once they return. Phase 1 has no sprinting and no jumping.
3. **Strength, 20–25 min.** The compound lifts, with two to three minutes between sets.
4. **Tissue work, 8–10 min.** Nordics, Copenhagens and calves. Last, and never cut.

## How the 12 weeks progress

Three phases of four weeks each. Volume first, load second, impact last. Tendon and bone adapt far more slowly than muscle, and they only remodel when loading genuinely drops, which a competitive season never allows. Achilles and patellar tendon problems in this family are almost always accumulated rather than sudden. Four weeks without impact is not lost training. It is the maintenance interval that makes the next three blocks possible.

| | Weeks 1–4 | Weeks 5–8 | Weeks 9–12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase** | Tendon recovery | Hypertrophy | Force and elasticity |
| **Goal** | Let tendons recover with zero impact loading | Build muscle and general maximal strength | Restore force production and reactive strength before sprinting resumes |
| **Looks like** | Machines, controlled ranges, higher reps, no impact | Free weights return, loads climb, low-level jumping comes back | Running returns gradually, starting at 70 per cent effort |
| **Working sets per week** | 59 | 64 | 68 |
| **Average effort** | RPE 6.2, about 4 reps in reserve | RPE 7.1, about 3 reps in reserve | RPE 7.7, about 2 reps in reserve |
| **Landings per week** | 0 | 100 | 68 |
| **Throws per week** | 0 | 0 | 20 |

RPE is effort rated out of 10. Reps in reserve is how many more good reps you could have done, so "about 3 reps in reserve" means about 3 reps left in the tank.

**Deload weeks:** weeks 6 and 12 are lighter, with the sets halved. Week 12 should hand you to the next Base block rested and heavier, not tired.

### The rules that make it work
- **Two weeks fully off, then four weeks with no impact.** No sprinting, no jumping and no plyometrics until week 5. This is the only real tendon-repair window in the calendar.
- **This is the block where you get bigger.** Higher reps, more volume, and permission to gain some weight. Muscle built in the off-season becomes force in the spring. Muscle chased in April becomes fatigue.
- **Volume, then load, then speed.** Reps climb in phase 1, weight climbs in phase 2, and running returns in phase 3. Even then it starts at 70 per cent and builds.
- **Fix the thing you ran through.** Every sprinter has something. Run the matching AXIS corrective program alongside phases 1 and 2, for the hamstring, Achilles, hip or lower back. There is no other twelve-week window like this in the year.
- **Finish rested and heavier, not tired.** An off-season that ends in exhaustion has failed at its one job.
- **Count your landings.** Every jump, hop, bound and drop counts, warm-up hops included. Phase 1 has none at all, and the warm-up uses calf raises instead. From week 5 the week stays under about 100 landings, with sport practice counted on top.

### Terms (expandable)
- **Block:** 12 weeks. This program. One block develops one set of qualities to a set standard.
- **Phase (mesocycle):** 4 weeks. Each one targets one quality. Four weeks is a choice, not a rule. The length follows the goal.
- **Training week (microcycle):** where effort climbs, sets are added and the deload lands.
- **Training year (macrocycle):** four blocks in order (Base, Build, Compete, Restore), about 48 weeks.

## How you'll measure progress

From week 5, once jumping returns, every week starts with a 90-second check: three standing broad jumps at the start of Day 1, keeping the best. More than 10 per cent below your own rolling average means that day is reduced. The power work comes out and each lift loses one set, whatever the page says.

**What good progress looks like**
- **Weeks 1–4:** the tendons quieten, morning stiffness fades, and training feels easy because it is meant to.
- **Weeks 5–8:** strength and muscle come back fast. This is where the year's body composition work actually happens.
- **Weeks 9–12:** sprinting returns gradually on legs that have had a genuine break.

Arriving at the next Base block heavier, stronger and out of pain is the whole objective.

## Respect the week

Count everything: sport, gym and sleep. Sudden jumps in weekly workload are the most reliable predictor of injury in amateur sport. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep. It is the most powerful performance tool available, and the cheapest.

## The science

Every program in the library is built on published research, and how strong that research is gets stated, not implied. Where the evidence is limited, the page says so.

| Finding | Source | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Lower-limb tendon pain responds to progressive loading. Which progression rule works best is not established. | Escriche-Escuder, Casaña & Cuesta-Vargas, *BMJ Open* 2020. Systematic review, 30 RCTs in Achilles, patellar and gluteal tendinopathy. PMID 33444210 | Most programs progress on pain, and that practice is not supported by strong evidence. Credit the loading, not a particular tempo. Eccentric-only work has not been shown to be superior. |
| Programs that include the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halve hamstring injuries. | van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley, *Br J Sports Med* 2019. Meta-analysis, 15 studies, 8,459 athletes. PMID 30808663 | Risk ratio 0.49. The effect shrinks but survives when the eight high-risk-of-bias studies are removed (0.55). It measures programs that include the exercise, not the exercise alone. |
| Strength training cuts sports injuries to under a third of the control rate. Stretching does not reduce them. | Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen, *Br J Sports Med* 2014. Meta-analysis of 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants. PMID 24100287 | Risk ratio 0.32 for strength training and 0.96 for stretching. The trials varied a lot, and dose was not standardised. |
| A single adductor exercise, done three times a week in pre-season and once a week in season, cut reported groin problems by 41%. | Harøy et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2019. Cluster-randomised trial, 35 teams, 652 male footballers. PMID 29891614 | Odds ratio 0.59. Self-reported problems in semi-professional men's football only. It has not been repeated in women or other sports. |
| Every sensible prescription beats not training. Heavier loads rank highest for strength. Multiple sets rank highest for muscle size. | Currier et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2023. Network meta-analysis, 178 strength and 119 hypertrophy studies. PMID 37414459 | The differences between prescriptions were small, and all of them worked. |
